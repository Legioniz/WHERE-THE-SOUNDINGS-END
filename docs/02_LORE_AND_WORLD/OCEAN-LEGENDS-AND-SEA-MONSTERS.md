## 1. Purpose and evidence rules

This document expands the project's maritime-lore catalogue with source-traceable examples from several ocean-going cultures and literary traditions. It is an inspiration and research document, not a claim that supernatural creatures exist and not an approved list of entities in the game's canon.

Keep these evidence categories separate:

- **Documented historical event:** a shipwreck, voyage, archival record, or archaeological discovery supported by historical evidence.
- **Historical testimony:** a person or publication reported an encounter. This establishes that the report circulated, not that the described monster existed.
- **Mythology / religious tradition:** a figure preserved in a mythic, religious, or literary corpus.
- **Oral tradition / living belief:** a culturally situated story that may have variants and remains meaningful to a community.
- **Literary fiction:** a deliberate fictional work, sometimes built from real history and folklore.
- **Scientific comparison:** a real animal or physical phenomenon that may help explain a reported sighting; it does not prove that a specific legend was caused by it.

A story can combine several categories. Record the category for each claim, not merely for the whole creature.

## 2. Whale legends, historic attacks, and the boundary between animal and monster

### 2.1 Mocha Dick and Moby-Dick — Pacific white whale

**Evidence:** historical testimony + literary fiction.

Mocha Dick was the name used in an 1839 account by Jeremiah N. Reynolds for a reportedly enormous, pale sperm whale encountered by whalers in the Pacific, near Mocha Island off Chile. The story described a whale that had survived repeated attacks. Herman Melville's *Moby-Dick; or, The Whale* (1851) drew on that account, his own whaling experience, and other whaling literature.

The novel's most important historical background is the whaleship *Essex*. On 20 November 1820, a sperm whale rammed the ship in the mid-Pacific and it sank. First mate Owen Chase published his account in 1821. The crew's long ordeal in open boats became part of the real-world context for Melville's voyage and its catastrophic ending.

**What is established:** the *Essex* disaster and Chase's account are historical; Melville's novel is fiction; Mocha Dick is a reported whale from a contemporary published account. Do not claim that the fictional Moby Dick was one proven animal or that every detail of Mocha Dick's story is independently verified.

**Design value:** a powerful animal need not be evil, intelligent in a human way, or supernatural. Give it a home range, scars, feeding behaviour, learned avoidance, and a reason to react to a vessel. The horror can come from human obsession, industrial extraction, and the scale of the ocean rather than a boss-monster attack.

### 2.2 Hafgufa and Lyngbakr — Norse sea beasts

**Evidence:** medieval literary / mythic tradition + modern scientific comparison.

The 13th-century Old Norse text *Konungs skuggsjá* (*The King's Mirror*) describes a huge sea creature called **hafgufa**. It was said to be so large that it could be mistaken for land. Its unusual feeding method involved opening its mouth and attracting fish with food-like material before closing its jaws around the gathering prey. The later *Örvar-Odds saga* also describes a **lyngbakr** (“heather-back”), another enormous sea creature associated with an island-like appearance.

A modern marine-mammal study compares the old hafgufa description with trap-feeding and tread-water feeding behaviours recorded in cetaceans. This is a striking example of how observations of unfamiliar animal feeding can become a sea-monster account. It does not prove that the medieval writer saw a particular whale species.

**Design value:** the player sees what looks like a reef, island, or dead patch of water; fish begin gathering around it; the Sounding Rig returns an impossible or slowly changing contour; only later does the “terrain” move. A gigantic filter-feeding or trap-feeding animal can be an ecosystem event rather than a conventional hostile encounter.

### 2.3 The Kraken — North Atlantic / Scandinavian maritime folklore

**Evidence:** folklore and later literary imagery.

The kraken is popularly imagined as an enormous tentacled cephalopod capable of attacking ships. That familiar form developed over time. Medieval Scandinavian sea-monster descriptions did not always use the word *kraken* or describe an octopus. The 13th-century hafgufa is a separate named creature in the medieval text tradition. Later natural-history writing, illustrations, and fiction helped consolidate the modern tentacled image.

Giant squid and other large cephalopods are real animals, but their existence does not establish the supernatural ship-destroying kraken.

**Design value:** use scale, suction, tentacle traces, damaged structures, disturbed sediment, and an animal's feeding route. Avoid making every tentacled creature an all-powerful ship killer.

### 2.4 Leviathan and Lotan — ancient Near Eastern sea chaos

**Evidence:** ancient religious and mythological texts.

Leviathan appears in poetic passages in the Hebrew Bible. Scholarship has connected the name and imagery with the Ugaritic / Canaanite sea serpent Lotan, often described as a twisting or crooked serpent and associated with chaos. Biblical passages do not present one consistent zoological creature: Leviathan can function as a force of chaos, a creature under divine power, or a poetic image; one passage is also interpreted in relation to a crocodile.

**Design value:** Leviathan is strongest as a thematic reference for the sea as an old, ordering force beyond human control—not as a routine fish, collectible, or boss with a health bar. Use it to shape lore about limits, forbidden depth, or a world that cannot be mastered.

### 2.5 Jörmungandr — Norse world serpent

**Evidence:** Norse mythic texts, written down after the Viking Age.

Jörmungandr is the world-serpent associated with the ocean surrounding Midgard. In the Ragnarök cycle it rises from the sea and meets Thor; the encounter is fatal to both. The surviving Eddic texts are crucial sources, but the written records postdate much of the period whose beliefs they preserve.

**Design value:** an entity whose scale is communicated through geography: a current that seems to encircle an island, a ring of distant disturbances, or a serpent-like route that cannot be seen in one camera view. Its use should remain cosmic and rare, not a creature that can be casually caught.

## 3. Dangerous passages, apparitions, and the haunted sea

### 3.1 Scylla and Charybdis — Greek literary mythology

**Evidence:** ancient Greek epic, especially Homer's *Odyssey*.

Scylla is a multi-headed monster living in a sea cave beside a dangerous passage. Charybdis is a violent whirlpool that threatens to swallow ships. Odysseus must sail between them, accepting that the passage offers no perfectly safe route. Later tradition localised the pair in the Strait of Messina, although the geography and interpretation of the Homeric account have been debated.

**Design value:** a navigation encounter built around two different dangers. One is predictable but costly; the other is less predictable and can destroy the vessel. The challenge is route choice and timing, not simply defeating both monsters.

### 3.2 The Flying Dutchman — phantom ship

**Evidence:** maritime folklore and later literature.

The Flying Dutchman is a ghost ship condemned to sail without making port. Its versions vary, and its popular literary history becomes visible around the late 18th and 19th centuries. Scholarly work has examined how the story was reshaped through Anglo-Dutch rivalry, colonial history, new technology, and repeated literary adaptation. Some reported distant ship apparitions near the Cape of Good Hope have been discussed as possible mirage effects; this is not a complete explanation for every version of the legend.

**Design value:** show a ship where no ship can be, far beyond safe range. It never needs to attack. The player may waste fuel chasing it, find an empty horizon, or later discover a real wreck in the direction it appeared. Keep the uncertainty grounded in visibility, weather, and navigational error.

### 3.3 Davy Jones and Davy Jones's Locker — sailors' folklore

**Evidence:** historical maritime language and folklore; origin uncertain.

“Davy Jones's locker” means the bottom of the sea or the place where drowned sailors end up. A clear 1751 literary reference describes Davy Jones as a sailor's fiend associated with evil spirits of the deep and approaching maritime disasters. The origin of the name is uncertain; popular claims that identify him with a specific historical pirate or a single real person are not established.

**Design value:** use the phrase as sailor vocabulary, a superstition, an old chart label, or a disputed explanation for missing crews. It does not need a literal Davy Jones character.

### 3.4 Umibōzu — Japanese sea apparition

**Evidence:** Japanese folklore documented in later collections and regional tellings.

Umibōzu is a sea apparition reported in Japanese maritime folklore. It may appear as a huge dark humanoid shape rising from the water, sometimes after a calm sea abruptly becomes dangerous. Some versions describe it demanding a bucket or barrel; the sailors escape by giving it a bottomless vessel. Accounts and depictions vary by region and source.

**Design value:** a horizon-level silhouette in calm water; a sudden change in sea state; a demand delivered through an impossible sound or radio transmission. Let the encounter be about surviving a rule, not dealing damage to a humanoid sea boss.

### 3.5 Sea serpents and the Gloucester reports

**Evidence:** historical testimony, printed investigation, and later interpretation.

Sea serpents appear in European and North Atlantic accounts for centuries. Olaus Magnus's 1539 *Carta Marina* and his 1555 writing helped circulate dramatic northern sea-monster imagery. In August 1817, many witnesses around Gloucester Harbor, Massachusetts, reported a long, serpent-like marine animal. The Linnaean Society of New England collected statements and investigated the reports. The descriptions were inconsistent and no new giant serpent species was scientifically established.

One real animal sometimes discussed in relation to sea-serpent sightings is the **oarfish**. It has a long ribbon-like silver body and red dorsal fin, lives in the ocean at depth, and is rarely seen alive at the surface. Museums note that its unusual appearance and occasional surface or beach encounters may have contributed to sea-serpent stories. This is a possible source of some reports, not a proven explanation for every sighting.

**Design value:** an encounter that is partly hidden by waves, distance, fog, or repeated surface breaks. Witnesses may disagree about its length, number of humps, or direction. The investigation can reveal a real rare fish, floating debris, a misread wake, or something that remains unexplained.

## 4. Japanese and Ainu sea traditions

### 4.1 Bake-kujira — ghost whale

**Evidence:** regional Japanese folklore, especially accounts associated with Izumo / Shimane; details need source-by-source verification.

Bake-kujira is described in modern folklore references as a skeletal whale appearing offshore, sometimes accompanied by strange fish and birds. Its appearance is treated as an omen associated with poor catches, disease, or bad weather in some tellings. A written depiction is associated with the 1841 *Ehon Hyaku Monogatari*. The tradition sits at the boundary between whale reverence, fishing livelihood, and supernatural warning.

**Design value:** an ecological omen rather than a combat encounter. The whale's appearance could precede a change in weather, unusual scavenger activity, or a temporary failure in fishing grounds. Avoid turning the skeletal whale into ordinary loot.

### 4.2 Akkorokamui — giant red octopus tradition

**Evidence:** Ainu-associated regional folklore; modern summaries require consultation with Ainu-authored sources before direct adaptation.

Akkorokamui is commonly described in modern summaries as a huge red octopus associated with Uchiura Bay in Hokkaido. Some accounts present the being as powerful or divine, not simply a monster. Descriptions and interpretations are not uniform.

**Design value:** a large animal-like presence associated with a particular bay, with a boundary that local fishers understand. Do not directly reuse the name or sacred identity as a boss, loot source, or generic “Japanese monster” without Ainu-specific consultation.

### 4.3 Ryūjin, Watatsumi, and the sea kami

**Evidence:** Japanese religious and folk practice, with distinct traditions and historical layers.

Ryūjin is a dragon or serpent-associated water deity connected with rain, rivers, deep pools, and the sea. The Kokugakuin University Encyclopedia of Shinto notes that fishermen prayed to Ryūjin for calm seas and abundant catches, and that local sea-deity practices should not be collapsed into a single version of the sea god in classical myth. Watatsumi is another major sea kami in Japanese mythic traditions, associated with an undersea palace.

**Design value:** a reciprocal relationship between fisher and sea: offerings, taboos, restraint, safe passage, and the expectation that taking from the water comes with obligations. These are stronger references for community customs and worldbuilding than for a hostile creature.

## 5. Māori and other Pacific traditions

### 5.1 Taniwha — Māori place-associated beings

**Evidence:** living Māori traditions; forms and roles vary by iwi, hapū, and locality.

Taniwha are associated with deep pools, caves, rivers, lakes, dangerous passages, and the sea. Some are feared as dangerous beings; others are kaitiaki (guardians) who protect particular communities or places. Te Ara's account describes Tuhirangi as a guardian associated with the navigator Kupe and Cook Strait, guiding and protecting canoes. The meaning of a taniwha is place-specific and cannot be reduced to “a Māori sea monster.”

**Design value:** a being whose territory is understood through local knowledge. It might guide a boat through a dangerous current, protect a reef, or become hostile when a place is violated. The important motif is a relationship between people, place, and responsibility—not a monster to kill.

### 5.2 Te Wheke-a-Muturangi / Te Wheke-o-Muturangi — giant octopus account

**Evidence:** Māori narrative tradition; spelling and details vary in retellings.

A giant octopus associated with Kupe's voyages appears in Māori story collections and later summaries. Some versions describe the pursuit or defeat of the octopus as part of the explanation for features of the New Zealand coastline. This should be researched through Māori-authored and iwi-specific sources before any direct use; do not treat one modern retelling as a universal Māori account.

**Design value:** a long-distance pursuit where currents, coastlines, and dangerous passages preserve evidence of something that travelled through them. The route and geography can be more important than the creature's final fight.

## 6. Arctic and North Atlantic boundary beings

### 6.1 Qalupalik / Qallupilluit — Inuit shoreline being

**Evidence:** Inuit oral traditions; versions vary by region and storyteller.

Qalupalik (with regional spelling variants) is a being associated with the sea and, in some stories, the underside of sea ice. It is often used in cautionary stories about children approaching dangerous shorelines or ice. Inuit-led storytelling and film sources preserve particular versions; the figure should not be generalised into a universal Inuit monster.

**Design value:** a boundary warning tied to a hazardous place and a community's survival knowledge. Its strongest role is as a story about listening, weather, ice, and distance—not as a random predator. The game's warm-water provisional setting is not a reason to transplant this figure directly.

### 6.2 Selkies — seal people of the North Atlantic

**Evidence:** Scottish, Orkney, Shetland, and wider North Atlantic folklore with many local variants.

Selkies transform between seal and human forms. One well-known story pattern involves a person hiding a selkie's skin and preventing them from returning to the sea; when the skin is found, the selkie returns home. The stories often explore captivity, longing, identity, family, and belonging rather than a sea-monster attack.

**Design value:** a missing-person or identity mystery, a human story attached to the shore, or a creature whose “escape” is actually a return home. Avoid making the selkie a collectible disguise or a combat enemy.

## 7. Caribbean, South American, and broader water traditions

### 7.1 Lusca — Bahamian blue-hole monster

**Evidence:** Bahamian folklore and modern retellings; no verified monster specimen.

Lusca is commonly described as a hybrid of shark and octopus, associated with blue holes and underwater caves around Andros in the Bahamas. Descriptions differ, and modern summaries sometimes add features that are not stable across accounts.

**Design value:** connect a local monster story to a real geological feature: a blue hole, tidal sink, cave system, or strong current. A player hears about something dragging divers into a dark opening, then must distinguish dangerous currents and cave geometry from the story.

### 7.2 Mami Wata — diverse African and diasporic water-spirit traditions

**Evidence:** a broad family of religious and artistic traditions, not one uniform creature.

Mami Wata traditions occur across diverse West and Central African and diasporic contexts. Images and stories may associate water spirits with beauty, wealth, danger, healing, power, or demanding relationships with humans. The name does not refer to one single standard character or one fixed pan-African myth.

**Design value:** a powerful non-human presence associated with exchange, desire, wealth, obligation, or danger. Use only broad narrative questions unless a specific community, tradition, and source are researched. Do not make a generic mermaid villain and call it Mami Wata.

### 7.3 Yacumama — Amazonian great water serpent

**Evidence:** Indigenous Amazonian traditions with specific community variants; not an ocean legend.

Yacumama (“mother of the water” in a commonly cited gloss) is associated in some Amazonian accounts with a great anaconda-like water being, guardian, or source of life. A 2025 scholarly article discusses a Desano account from the upper Vaupés basin. This is a river-world tradition, not a generic South American sea monster.

**Design value:** useful only if the game explores river mouths, inland waterways, or the border between freshwater and sea. Attribute it to a specific people and source; do not use “Amazonian” as if it names one culture.

## 8. Philippine maritime lore and local knowledge

### 8.1 Bakunawa — moon-swallowing serpent

**Evidence:** Philippine folklore with regional variants and community-specific tellings.

Bakunawa is widely known in Philippine folklore as a serpent or dragon associated with swallowing the moon and causing an eclipse. It is not simply a deep-sea predator. The National Museum of the Philippines documents an Abaknon account in which a lunar eclipse is explained through the moon being swallowed by a serpent called bakunawa. The same account records detailed Abaknon knowledge of moon phases, tides, current directions, and the timing of fishing methods.

A 2025 academic study analyses the Bakunawa legend as a narrative that encodes pattern, sequence, spatial reasoning, and cultural knowledge. Do not assume the Abaknon account is identical to all Visayan or Filipino versions.

**Design value:** connect the sea, moon phase, tide, current, and fishing calendar. An eclipse-like event may change navigation, fish behaviour, or community activity. The gongs and noise associated with some tellings can inspire a fictional community-wide warning ritual, but do not copy a specific community's practice without verifying its context.

### 8.2 Kataw and Visayan sea people

**Evidence:** historical accounts of Visayan belief as analysed in modern scholarship; historical descriptions are not evidence of a biological species.

A modern Cambridge study of Southeast Asian sea cosmologies discusses early accounts by the Jesuit chronicler Francisco Ignacio Alcina. The article describes beings called **kataw** in Visayan accounts as male and female fish-people or marine humanoids, distinguished in that account from dugongs and from European mermaid imagery. This is a historical record of what an author reported about local belief; it is not zoological evidence and should not be generalised across all Visayan communities.

**Design value:** an old coastal account, a disputed fisher sighting, a figure in a carved post, or a story about people seen sunning themselves on a low island. Prefer a newly designed fictional local presence and precise attribution over copying the name.

### 8.3 Sea anito, reef guardians, and maritime offerings

**Evidence:** historical and anthropological accounts of specific Southeast Asian and Philippine maritime communities.

Research on Southeast Asian maritime cosmologies records historical references to sea-associated spirits and offerings connected with fisheries, navigation, rocks, reefs, capes, and shoreline places. A separate 2024 study of Filipino seafarers documents how beliefs and superstitions continue to be carried into modern shipboard life, connecting maritime work with local community practices.

**Design value:** the human culture surrounding the sea can be as compelling as the monster catalogue. Give Greyhaven fishers different rules for whistles, names spoken at night, taking from a reef, returning the first catch, or passing a dangerous point. These should be invented for the game's fictional community unless tied to a properly sourced local tradition; never present a designed ritual as a universal Filipino practice.

These are **motif recommendations**, not direct imports of named beings into the game's canon.

### 9.1 Best design lessons

1. **A monster can be an animal first.** Mocha Dick and hafgufa offer more believable ecology than an enemy that exists only to attack the player.
2. **A warning can be more frightening than an attack.** Umibōzu, the Flying Dutchman, and Bake-kujira are useful for building anticipation and interpretation.
3. **A place can have agency in local belief.** Taniwha and Japanese sea-kami traditions provide a model for location-specific relationships and obligations.
4. **Navigation can be the encounter.** Scylla and Charybdis demonstrate a danger where route choice matters more than combat.
5. **The community's customs make the sea feel inhabited.** Bakunawa-related moon knowledge and specific seafarer beliefs can shape fishing calendars, dialogue, warnings, and local knowledge.
6. **Not every legend should become a creature.** Some belong in songs, chart annotations, ship names, memorials, taboos, rumours, or the way an NPC refuses to sail after dark.

## 10. Cultural and factual safeguards

- Keep this document non-canon until the World Bible accepts a specific fictional adaptation.
- Do not present the Moby Dick whale as a verified animal or confuse the *Essex* disaster with the fictional *Pequod*.
- Do not collapse the medieval hafgufa, later kraken imagery, and real giant squid into one creature.
- Do not treat Leviathan, Ryūjin, Watatsumi, taniwha, Mami Wata, or other religious / culturally significant beings as generic combat bosses.
- Taniwha, Qalupalik, Ainu-associated Akkorokamui, Bakunawa, kataw, Mami Wata, and Yacumama all need exact community and regional context. A living tradition should not be stripped of its meaning for an enemy skin or loot table.
- Do not claim that the oarfish explains every historical sea-serpent sighting.
- A historical testimony proves that a report was made; it does not prove the reported monster existed.
- When direct representation is considered, seek community-authored sources and consultation where appropriate. Prefer original fictional composites built from broad motifs.
