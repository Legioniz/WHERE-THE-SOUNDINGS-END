# Island and Settlement History

## 1. Historical approach

The island chain has no approved real-world location, calendar system or final settlement chronology. This document gives the story a coherent sequence without pretending that every date, cause or population figure is known.

The physical setting is a volcanic island arc with a sheltered bay, exposed basalt headlands, a shifting shallow shelf, offshore ridges and a deep-water margin. Shorelines, sediment channels and safe passages change over time. The same chart can be accurate for the day it was made and unsafe years later.

Use the labels **older**, **generations ago**, **eleven years before the opening** and **eight years before the opening** until an exact in-world calendar is approved.

## 2. Before Greyhaven

The oldest history is geological rather than political. Volcanic construction, erosion, slope failure, sediment deposition and water-level change shape the island chain. The Hollow Trench and offshore ridges are part of this physical setting; they are not proof of supernatural design.

People settled the sheltered bay because it provided a protected anchorage, nearshore food, access to freshwater and a route toward offshore fishing grounds. The first settlement grew around practical access to the shore rather than around a mystery.

The names of the earliest inhabitants, original language, first landing date and first formal belief practices remain undecided. Do not assign an invented people to a real-world Indigenous or historical culture.

## 3. The lower settlement and the Drowned Parish

Before the present village consolidated around the higher harbour edge, people occupied a lower coastal district closer to the old inlet. The district included working houses, storage, a landing place, a communal record house and a bell structure used for local notices or gathering. Its exact religious and administrative status is not yet defined.

Over generations, the low ground became harder to maintain. Shoreline migration, repeated storm damage, changing channels and local ground movement are the proposed physical pressures. Families relocated in stages to higher ground. Some foundations, walls and rooflines were left below ordinary high water; other materials were salvaged and reused in the present settlement.

Mariners later called the submerged remains **the Drowned Parish**. The name describes a place and a memory, not a confirmed single catastrophic flood. Accounts disagree about which structures were abandoned first and whether the old bell structure was still standing when the district was vacated.

The Parish's bell, if it survives, is **not automatically the same bell as the lighthouse bell**. This distinction is essential. A sound heard over the water may be attributed to either in local stories, but the physical instruments have separate histories unless a future approved record proves otherwise.

## 4. Greyhaven becomes the working harbour

The newer settlement developed along the more sheltered and better-drained shore. The harbour grew around a practical division of labour:
- the Harbour Office records the channel, moorings, watch and warnings;
- the fish market receives and grades catches, arranges ice and keeps accounts;
- mechanics keep engines, hulls and fittings serviceable;
- net workers maintain gear and preserve useful materials;
- the inn gives crews a place to wait for weather, work and news.

The west headland lighthouse became a reliable visual bearing. Its bell provided an audible warning when visibility or distance made the light difficult to read. The precise construction date, original funder and keeper succession remain open.

The settlement retains material from the older district. Reused timber, stone, glass, metal fittings and repaired tools should appear as ordinary continuity, not as magical relics.

## 5. Eleven years before the opening — the last confirmed lighthouse bell

The Harbourmaster's log records the last confirmed sounding of the lighthouse bell eleven years before the current story. Later entries record inspections, not another confirmed ring.

The fact of the silence is established in the current dialogue prototype. The reason is not. Possible explanations include a damaged mechanism, a maintenance decision, a storm incident, a change in navigation practice or an unexplained report that caused the keeper to stop using it. These are competing research leads, not simultaneous facts.

The lighthouse bell's silence predates the Orison loss by three years. It must not be explained as a response to the Orison.

## 6. Eight years before the opening — the Orison loss

The survey vessel Orison was operating near the deep-water route later associated with the Hollow Trench. The ship recorded a delayed sounding return and radio material that appeared to contain a familiar private call. Its captain ordered the crew to reduce responses and divert away from the original survey line.

The vessel was lost. The Sounder was the only person recovered. No intact wreck or definitive remains were found. The official explanation relied on navigational error, equipment failure and severe conditions. Those factors may describe part of the incident but do not resolve all the surviving evidence.

The Orison's sponsor, survey mandate, full crew list, precise last position and final route remain unapproved. Do not invent a named corporation, military programme or conspiracy to fill those gaps.

## 7. The present incident

At the opening of the game, three boats have travelled beyond the outer marker. One returns. Two remain missing. The returned skipper is exhausted and incomplete as a witness; the question about the bell is the most consistent detail in their account.

A sounding buoy washes in near the western mudflat. The Harbourmaster asks the player to recover it without switching it on. This practical job brings the protagonist into the current investigation without requiring them to announce a supernatural theory.

Mara, Tomas and Inez contribute separate observations. Their records must remain occupationally specific:
- catch temperature and storage;
- hull scoring and mechanical inspection;
- net condition, knot integrity and handling;
- Harbourmaster log dates and channel records.

The four records do not prove that one cause produced all four observations.

## 8. Historical evidence still needed

A production pass should eventually author:
- a dated series of Harbour Office logs around the bell's final service;
- a small set of Drowned Parish records with original and later annotations distinguished;
- one older survey account that predates the Orison;
- the Orison's route fragment and instrument record;
- an independent source for Nell's radio actions;
- a public report of the present three-boat incident;
- personal effects that connect missing crews to ordinary lives.

Every record should have a credible author, date range, purpose and reason for being preserved. Do not let a document exist only because the player needs exposition.

## Regional histories as one connected chain

The six regions form one physical and human geography connected by geology, migration, fisheries, salvage, changing routes and copied records. Greyhaven grew uphill from the lower inlet; the Shallows became the first navigation school; Blackrock shaped seamanship through surge and reflected sound; the Outer Shoals developed repeated survey traditions; the Drowned Parish preserves the material traces of staged relocation; and the Hollow Trench is known chiefly through increasingly uncertain instruments. Use the relative chronology in TIMELINE.md. The lighthouse bell's last confirmed sounding remains Year -11; the Orison was lost at Year -8. The Parish bell remains a separate object.
