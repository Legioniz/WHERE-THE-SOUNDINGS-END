## Purpose

This document records how the first Deep Research report should be used. Its goal is to give the team a creative starting point without confusing mythology, oral tradition, disputed historical claims, and marine science.

## What the returned report contains

The report supplied:
- A summary of maritime folklore motifs across several regions.
- A motif table covering sirens, kraken, Leviathan, drowned kingdoms, taniwha, Mami Wata, Kamohoaliʻi, selkies, umibōzu, Qalupalik, Yacumama, Yawkyawk, Scylla and Charybdis, Makara, and Arctic revenant / ghost-ship motifs.
- Six expanded example quest hooks.
- A list of twelve proposed quest filenames.
- A seasonal event calendar concept.
- Audio and visual suggestions for acoustic, fog, bell, whale-song, and bioluminescent cues.

The initial research brief asked for a much larger, deeply sourced catalogue. The returned report is a useful first pass, but it does **not** provide thirty fully evidenced entries or a sufficiently complete primary-source bibliography. Do not present it as exhaustive research.

## Evidence labels

Use these labels in any future research expansion:

- **Documented history / archaeology:** supported by material evidence, dated records, or a recognized scholarly project.
- **Historical testimony:** a record that someone reported an event; the report itself does not prove the interpretation.
- **Mythology / literary tradition:** a figure or event appearing in a text or established mythic corpus.
- **Oral tradition / living belief:** attribute to the specific community and source; variants may differ.
- **Modern folklore:** a story or motif circulating in later popular culture.
- **Scientific phenomenon:** supported by scientific observation; fictional use must preserve its actual constraints.
- **Disputed / unsupported:** claims that need strong qualification or should not be used as fact.

## Source-quality assessment of the first report

The initial report cites a mixture of general reference pages, Wikipedia articles, and secondary websites. Those links are starting points only. They are not sufficient by themselves for final claims about sacred traditions, archaeological history, or the details of living Indigenous cultures.

In particular:
- A general article about sea monsters does not establish that a specific encounter occurred.
- A popular blog about the kraken is not an authoritative basis for historical claims.
- Wikipedia can help identify terminology and further references, but should not be the final citation for sensitive cultural or historical content.
- A general summary of Mami Wata cannot stand in for the diverse communities and religious contexts in which the traditions appear.
- The existence and meaning of a taniwha, Qalupalik, Kamohoaliʻi, Makara, Yacumama, or Yawkyawk account must be tied to a specific, trustworthy source and community context. Avoid universal claims.
- The supplied calendar months and seasonal events are game-design proposals, not verified global maritime customs or a scientifically valid universal seasonal cycle.

## Cultural-use requirements

Before direct depiction of any named figure, sacred being, ritual, or culturally significant place:
1. Identify the exact community, region, language, and version of the tradition.
2. Find community-authored sources, cultural institutions, museums, university research, or primary texts where appropriate.
3. Check whether the material is sacred, restricted, private, or not intended for entertainment adaptation.
4. Seek consultation and permission when the project moves beyond broad, non-specific inspiration.
5. Do not make a named figure into a boss, collectible, upgrade, or source of loot by default.
6. Record what was changed, why, and which sources informed the fictional adaptation.

A fictional composite inspired by a general maritime motif is preferred when direct representation is not essential. Even then, avoid copying distinctive names, sacred practices, or culturally specific designs.

## Creative-use checklist

For every research-inspired quest, record:
- [ ] Evidence label and source links
- [ ] Exact cultural / geographic attribution
- [ ] What is documented versus uncertain
- [ ] What is borrowed only as a broad motif
- [ ] What has been invented for this game
- [ ] Cultural sensitivity review status
- [ ] Gameplay systems and dependencies
- [ ] Canon approval status
- [ ] Any claims that still need verification

## Canon boundary

Everything in the maritime research collection is **Proposed / Non-canon**. The World Bible remains authoritative. No real-world legend or historical claim automatically becomes part of Greyhaven's fictional history.

## Expanded source audit — November 10, 2026

A new [Ocean Legends and Sea Monsters research expansion](OCEAN-LEGENDS-AND-SEA-MONSTERS.md) adds a larger collection of individually sourced dossiers. The most useful improvements are:

- NOAA, the Smithsonian, and the University of Nebraska digital edition are used to distinguish the historical *Essex* disaster, the reported Mocha Dick, and Melville's fictional whale.
- A 13th-century Norse account of hafgufa is compared with a peer-reviewed study of cetacean trap-feeding; the comparison is suggestive, not proof of the creature's identity.
- Sea-serpent reports are treated as historical testimony, while museum sources explain how real oarfish may have contributed to some sightings without explaining all of them.
- Māori, Inuit, Ainu-associated, Philippine, and other living traditions receive explicit attribution and adaptation cautions.
- The Bakunawa section uses the National Museum of the Philippines' Abaknon material and a 2025 scholarly study, while warning that one community account is not a universal Filipino version.
- Japanese sea-deity material uses the Kokugakuin University Encyclopedia of Shinto to distinguish Ryūjin / local sea-kami worship from a generic monster profile.

The expansion still does not constitute an exhaustive global folklore survey. Several community-specific traditions need primary-language research and consultation before direct representation. The catalogue remains non-canon; source links document stories and beliefs, not the biological existence of monsters.
