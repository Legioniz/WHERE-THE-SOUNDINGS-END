# Local Ocean Stories and Mythology

> Project: WHERE THE SOUNDINGS END  
> Status: Proposed original in-world folklore — non-factual unless corroborated  
> Owner: Project lead  
> Version: 0.2  
> Last reviewed: 2026-11-14

## 1. Purpose and cultural boundary

This document creates a small original body of stories that could plausibly circulate among Greyhaven crews. It does not adapt a named deity, sacred being or living Indigenous tradition. The wider [Maritime Myths Catalogue](MARITIME-MYTHS-CATALOG.md) remains a non-canon research resource.

The island chain's exact language, real-world location and cultural history are not approved. Do not assign these stories to a real community or use them to imply that a real culture believes the Choir exists.

Local mythology should feel varied. Some residents tell stories for amusement; some use them as warnings; some reject them; some remember a practical event that acquired a supernatural explanation over time.

## 2. The Bell Under the Parish

**Common telling:** On a quiet turn of tide, a bell can sometimes be heard from below the old settlement. The sound is said to come once, pause, then come again from a different direction. People disagree about whether it means the water is high enough to cross the old road or whether a storm is coming.

**Alternative explanations:** a buoy, a hull fitting, a distant vessel, a whale call, wind through a structure, or memory altered by repetition.

**What the player can verify:** the Drowned Parish contains bell-related architecture and historical records. The existence, location and operation of a submerged bell must be established by physical evidence; a voice or sound alone is not enough.

**Continuity rule:** this story concerns the Parish's old bell tradition, not automatically the Greyhaven lighthouse bell.

## 3. The Wrong Light

**Common telling:** A light seen beyond the outer marker is sometimes said to wait for a boat to change course. Crews are told to use a known landmark and not steer solely toward a distant light.

**Alternative explanations:** a moving vessel, fog scatter, reflection, a misidentified marker, an unreliable chart or a light seen from an unexpected angle.

**What the player can verify:** navigation lights and visual bearings can be tested against known landmarks. The story should never hide a required hazard warning or make the player's controls unreliable.

## 4. The Empty Net

**Common telling:** A crew may haul a net that is still tied, still weighted and still properly set, but contains no catch. The oldest versions blame poor water, bad timing or a place that "keeps what it is given." Later versions add an unseen hand.

**Alternative explanations:** a failed set, shifted current, net avoidance, gear fouling, fish migration, an incorrect depth or a crew's memory of what was deployed.

**What the player can verify:** Inez's record concerns a specific net returned empty but not torn, with the knots still set. That is a physical observation, not proof of an unseen hand.

## 5. The Three Turns

**Common telling:** Some crews use three turns around a float line before leaving the harbour. A story says a line tied three times will remember the way home.

**Practical meaning:** the three-turn tie is a working habit used by one crew and recognised by Inez. It is not a universal local ritual and should not become a magical protection mechanic.

**Character meaning:** Inez respects the hands that tied the knot and the person who taught the technique. Her craft is about care, inspection and continuity.

## 6. The Spare Margin

**Common telling:** An old chartmaker's saying advises leaving one margin of a chart unfilled. It is a reminder that a blank area is more honest than a false line.

**Practical meaning:** charts contain surveyed, estimated and unsurveyed information. A careful mariner marks the confidence and source of a position rather than presenting every gap as known.

**Narrative use:** this saying can echo the final act of marking the last reliable sounding. It is a human principle, not a supernatural instruction.

## 7. The Boat That Waited

**Common telling:** A vessel is said to have returned to the same mooring after a long absence, with its lines still neatly coiled and no crew aboard. Different tellers claim it was found in different harbours.

**Status:** deliberately unstable rumour. No confirmed vessel, date or wreck is attached to the story at this stage.

**Narrative use:** a tavern or radio rumour can introduce the idea of a returning vessel without making a phantom ship a fact of the setting.

## 8. How stories change

A story may change when:
- a witness repeats a frightening detail and omits ordinary context;
- a practical warning becomes a superstition;
- a later crew borrows a name from an older tale;
- an official report removes emotional language but also omits uncertainty;
- a family remembers an absent person differently from a workmate.

The game should make these changes visible through differences between testimony, records and material evidence.

## 9. Use in quests and encounters

- No story above should become a literal boss by default.
- A rumour may lead to a location or testable observation, not a guaranteed supernatural reward.
- A local story can be partially true, wrong in its details or impossible to settle.
- The player journal should record what was found and keep the teller's interpretation separate.
- Cultural material from the separate research catalogue requires specific sourcing and review before direct depiction.
