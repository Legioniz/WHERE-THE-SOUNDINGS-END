# Deep-Ocean Phenomena and Mariner Experiences

> Project: WHERE THE SOUNDINGS END  
> Status: Proposed research-to-gameplay specification — not canon until reviewed  
> Owner: Project lead  
> Version: 0.1  
> Last reviewed: 2026-11-10

## 1. Purpose

This document converts the supplied deep-ocean and mariner-experience notes into usable world content. It is not simply a monster catalogue. The strongest material is frightening because the ocean is difficult to interpret: sonar can mistake a living layer for terrain, a current can look like a boundary, a vessel can make unfamiliar sounds, and fatigue can make ambiguous signals feel meaningful.

Build from three sources of unease:

1. **The real sea:** biology, geology, weather, sound propagation, and maritime hazards.
2. **The human sea:** isolation, fatigue, monotonous work, uncertain navigation, equipment failure, drifting vessels, and conflicting witness accounts.
3. **The fictional anomaly:** a small number of impossible details that cannot be fully explained by the first two categories.

The player must be able to investigate the first two without being forced to accept the third.

## 2. Evidence and canon rules

Every research-derived entry must carry an evidence label.

| Label | Meaning | Permitted use |
|---|---|---|
| Established science | Repeatable observation or strong scientific literature | Ordinary ecology, hazards, instrument behaviour |
| Documented maritime practice / event | Reliable records, operational documentation, historical evidence | Working routines, wrecks, navigation, human risk |
| Reported account | A witness account exists, but the claimed cause is not independently established | Testimony, rumor, log entry, disputed clue |
| Folklore / cultural tradition | A culturally situated narrative with local variants | Archive and respectful worldbuilding unless researched and approved |
| Unverified / unsupported claim | No reliable traceable source supplied, or claim conflicts with established knowledge | Never present as real-world fact; may inspire clearly fictional material |
| Fictional anomaly | An authored impossibility belonging to this game | In-world uncertainty, never real science |

A Reddit thread, social post, or video narration proves that a story circulated; it does not prove the described event happened as stated. Record original source and date where possible. Do not invent a named witness or official confirmation.

Research is not canon. A real phenomenon may be adapted, but game-specific behaviour, location, scale, and consequence remain proposed until approved in the World Bible and relevant system document.

## 3. Deep-ocean phenomena suitable for the world

### 3.1 Deep scattering layer and false seabed

**Evidence:** Established science. The deep scattering layer (DSL) is a concentration of organisms in the water column that reflect sonar. Many organisms perform diel vertical migration, moving upward at night to feed and downward during daylight. Early sonar operators sometimes interpreted the changing return as a moving seafloor.

**Placement:** The Outer Shoals and Hollow Trench approach.

**Player experience:** A broad return appears above the charted bottom; its depth changes between day and night; a second, weaker return reveals the actual seabed. At night, the layer rises; at dawn it descends. Fish, squid, jellies, and predators may be distributed along it.

**Rules:**
- The layer is a volume of organisms, not a solid floor or collision surface.
- Depth and density vary with light, local ecology, season, and water conditions.
- It must not teleport as a supernatural event during ordinary operation.
- A second ping, changed angle/frequency, chart comparison, or waiting can help distinguish it from the true bottom.
- A later impossible return is a separate fictional anomaly with its own event ID.

### 3.2 Brine pools

**Evidence:** Established science. Dense hypersaline water can collect in seafloor depressions, forming a sharply bounded pool. Its density inhibits mixing with surrounding seawater. Some pools have severe chemical and oxygen gradients and support specialised microbial communities.

**Placement:** An optional deep-basin survey site in the Hollow Trench.

**Player experience:** The Sounding Rig detects a sharp boundary close to the bottom. A camera shows a distinct interface and animals avoiding the pool. Scavengers or specialised organisms may live around its margin.

**Rules:**
- The pool is denser than surrounding seawater and contained by seabed topography.
- Do not depict it as a freshwater lake or an open-air liquid surface.
- Ordinary fish do not casually swim through it; entry is a serious physiological hazard.
- The boat is not swallowed by the pool. It is a seabed-scale hazard for a diver, ROV, or bottom-contact tool.
- Equipment danger requires explicit boundary interaction; proximity alone is not lethal.
- Avoid instant “pickling” animation. Use distress and rapid failure only for organisms that cross into unsuitable chemistry.

### 3.3 Hydrothermal vents and cold seeps

**Evidence:** Established science; these are distinct systems. Hydrothermal vents release magma-heated, mineral-rich fluids and can form chimneys. Cold seeps release fluids such as methane or hydrogen sulfide at temperatures close to surrounding seawater. Both can support chemosynthetic communities, but their chemistry, heat, stability, and ecology differ.

**Placement:** Black Chimneys (vent field) and Sulfur Orchard (cold seep), already proposed in the regional ecology.

**Player experience:** Vent plumes distort visibility and build mineral chimneys. The Sounding Rig returns dense vertical structures. Cold seeps show microbial mats, carbonate structures, tubeworms, and slow-moving scavengers. Both attract specialised wildlife but are not automatically hostile.

**Rules:**
- Never call a cold seep a hydrothermal vent or vice versa.
- Vent fluid may be extremely hot at its source, but surrounding seawater remains under pressure; do not portray the whole area as boiling.
- Danger comes from temperature gradients, chemistry, plume exposure, unstable substrate, and poor navigation.
- Seep organisms are adapted to their local chemistry; their presence does not mean the site is safe for the player.
- Do not populate every vent with identical giant worms and glowing fish.

### 3.4 Gas hydrates

**Evidence:** Established science. Gas hydrates are ice-like crystalline structures in which water molecules cage gas molecules. Marine deposits are commonly methane hydrate; other gases, including carbon dioxide, can form hydrates under suitable pressure and temperature conditions.

**Placement:** A deep-slope or trench-margin survey site associated with a cold seep or sediment scar.

**Player experience:** Pale ice-like deposits appear in sediment cracks. A disturbed deposit releases bubbles or changes the acoustic texture of the seabed. A survey note warns that the structure is stable only under local pressure and temperature conditions.

**Rules:**
- Do not describe gas hydrates as ordinary frozen volcanic exhaust.
- Do not show open-water instant freezing as a general reaction.
- Hydrate exposure is local to the seabed and supported by geology.
- A methane release may be a hazard or geological clue, but does not automatically cause a tsunami or ship-destroying explosion.
- Methane hydrate is the default reference unless a specific CO2 hydrate site is authored.

### 3.5 Brinicles

**Evidence:** Established physical phenomenon, geographically restricted. In polar sea ice, dense brine can descend and freeze surrounding seawater, forming a hollow ice tube beneath the ice. It may reach the seabed and trap small, slow-moving organisms.

**Placement:** Excluded from the current warm-water island-arc baseline. Reserve for a future polar expedition, archive footage, or a separate region if world geography changes.

**Rules:**
- Do not place brinicles in Greyhaven, The Shallows, Blackrock Reach, or the current warm-water Hollow Trench.
- A brinicle is not an underwater icicle that appears anywhere in deep water.
- Its danger is a local advancing freezing plume beneath sea ice, not an ocean-wide freezing beam.

### 3.6 Bioluminescence

**Evidence:** Established science. Many deep-sea organisms produce light for communication, camouflage, warning, or predation. The deep scattering layer contains numerous organisms with different body plans and behaviours.

**Placement:** Outer Shoals night migration, Lantern Drift, and deep-water survey locations.

**Player experience:** A brief blue-white flash follows an animal; disturbed water activates luminous plankton; individual points move at different speeds and depths. A predator may use a lure, but most lights are not lures and most luminous animals are not dangerous.

**Rules:**
- Every light has a plausible organism or physical trigger unless explicitly marked anomalous.
- Do not use radioactive green as the default colour.
- A glowing trail does not prove a giant creature is present.
- Light patterns can provide ecological information but are not a guaranteed threat detector.

### 3.7 Deep-sea gigantism and unusual body plans

**Evidence:** Established science, but not a universal deep-sea rule. Some deep-sea groups include unusually large species relative to shallow-water relatives. The pattern varies by lineage and environment; pressure alone is not a universal cause.

**Placement:** Hollow Trench and deep slope.

**Rules:**
- Every large animal needs a plausible food source, growth strategy, habitat, and movement budget.
- Do not assume all deep-sea organisms are giant.
- Size does not automatically imply aggression.
- Use slow movement, energy conservation, ambush, scavenging, and long resting periods where appropriate.
- Colossal squid and giant isopods are real reference animals; they do not validate exaggerated internet claims.

### 3.8 Osedax and whale falls

**Evidence:** Established science. Osedax are specialised worms that colonise whale bones. They lack a conventional mouth and digestive tract; root-like tissues penetrate bone, while symbiotic bacteria help access nutrients.

**Placement:** Bone Orchard, an existing proposed whale-fall site.

**Player experience:** A skeleton supports successive scavenger communities. Osedax colonies appear as small root-like growths on bone. Scavengers arrive, feed, reproduce, and leave as the food resource changes. A survey reveals that a whale fall is an ecosystem, not a static prop.

**Rules:**
- Osedax are not free-swimming predators or humanoid “zombie worms.”
- Their role is slow decomposition and nutrient transfer.
- A whale fall changes over time; scavenger abundance follows available tissue and bone resources.
- Protected whale remains cannot enter ordinary catch or sale inventory.
- Disturbing the site can alter the ecological record and trigger a conservation or investigation consequence.

## 4. Physical anomalies and claims requiring correction

The supplied notes contain dramatic structures and claims that should not be added as established facts without traceable primary evidence.

| Supplied claim | Evidence assessment | Project treatment |
|---|---|---|
| Perfectly straight “Mid-Atlantic Lineations” with rectangular holes spaced four inches apart | No reliable scientific source supplied; precision and discovery story are unverified | Do not state as a real mapped feature. May inspire a fictional survey anomaly with explicit in-world provenance |
| A 49-foot isolated reflective wall northwest of Hawaii | No reliable source supplied to establish the exact object, dimensions, or explanation | Exclude as real-world fact; a fictional basalt dyke can be designed independently if geology supports it |
| Massive volcanic CO2 structures that instantly freeze from boiling gas | Misleading mechanism; gas hydrates form under suitable pressure / temperature conditions, and methane is the common marine hydrate | Use the gas-hydrate rules above |
| Every brine pool is an instant death trap for any animal | Overgeneralised; chemistry and biological tolerance vary by site | Model a strong local chemical boundary and species-specific tolerance |
| Every deep-sea species becomes gigantic | False generalisation | Use species-specific size and ecology |
| Brinicles occur in ordinary tropical deep water | Incorrect geographic transfer | Polar-only unless the setting is redesigned |

A fictional structure may use these images, but its archive entry must say who observed it, with what instrument, at what coordinates, and what remains unverified.

## 5. Fishermen, deck crews, and human maritime horror

### 5.1 Mechanical defiance and line tension

**Evidence:** The “straightened hook” is a reported account in the supplied material; ordinary line failure and gear deformation are real maritime possibilities.

**Game use:** A longline returns with missing bait, a bent hook, a cut leader, or a line abraded against rock. The player inspects the gear before deciding that something impossibly strong pulled it.

**Rules:** Resolve plausible causes first: overload, snag, abrasion, corrosion, knot failure, fatigue, or a large animal. A genuinely impossible result is rare and requires corroborating evidence. Do not use “unbreakable alloy” as a factual material claim. One bent hook never proves a monster exists.

### 5.2 Hull knocks and underwater sound

**Evidence:** Reported account; ordinary hull noises have many possible causes.

**Game use:** Knocks may come from floating debris, mooring lines, thermal movement, loose equipment, fish, waves, or an unknown source.

**Rules:** Begin with a plausible mechanical or environmental source. Sound location may be uncertain because water and hull structures transmit vibration. A knock alone does not change threat state. A repeated pattern may become an investigation clue, but not a guaranteed encounter.

### 5.3 Sudden water boundaries

**Evidence:** Water masses, fronts, temperature / salinity transitions, runoff plumes, and current boundaries are established; the razor-sharp supernatural boundary described in the supplied account is not verified.

**Game use:** A visible colour or temperature line crosses a fishing ground. The player records temperature, salinity, turbidity, current, and wildlife response. Later, a fictional boundary may contradict one or more readings.

**Rules:** Natural fronts have width, drift, and environmental context; they are not vertical walls. A colour change alone is not proof of danger. Navigation remains physically continuous. A supernatural boundary requires its own anomaly ID and evidence chain.

### 5.4 Empty or drifting vessels

**Evidence:** Abandoned and derelict vessels are real maritime hazards; a particular ghost-ship story remains a report until verified.

**Game use:** A drifting boat with personal belongings, a running light, a damaged mooring, or an incomplete logbook. It can be a rescue, salvage, investigation, or environmental hazard.

**Rules:** Drift follows wind, current, waves, and damage unless an explicit anomaly is active. Boarding requires safe approach, stable relative motion, and a return route. Preserve rescue-first logic when a survivor may be present. An abandoned vessel is neither automatically supernatural nor automatically safe. Naval submarine sightings are not a routine fishing event; do not present anonymous claims as common operational fact.

### 5.5 Unexplained calls and acoustic anomalies

**Evidence:** Named internet sounds such as “Merfrog” and some “Goliath Bark” stories are not adequately sourced in the supplied material. Fish, mammals, ice, ships, and geological events do produce strong underwater sounds.

**Game use:** A hydrophone records a sound that can be compared with known engine, propeller, animal, ice, and geological profiles. The player may later find a plausible source—or evidence that does not fit.

**Rules:** A sound profile has frequency, duration, repetition, direction confidence, and environmental context. Never assign a biological source solely because a sound resembles a voice. A grouper-like boom may inspire a defensive fish sound, but the supplied claim that it physically stuns deckhands or vibrates a boat deck is not accepted as fact. “Quackers” and “Merfrog” remain reported internet / military lore unless primary records are found.

### 5.6 Milky seas and luminous wakes

**Evidence:** Bioluminescent surface events are reported and scientifically studied; the supplied submarine “illuminated hull” account is not established as routine.

**Game use:** A large patch of persistent surface glow may reveal microbial activity, current transport, or an unusual ecological event. A vessel wake briefly lights the water.

**Rules:** Use a plausible surface-scale phenomenon; do not automatically illuminate deep water for miles. Distinguish natural luminous water from a fictional anomaly. A glowing wake reveals the player's position only if a gameplay system explicitly supports that consequence.

### 5.7 Floating debris and disturbing salvage

**Evidence:** Marine debris is documented; the specific freezer story is anecdotal.

**Game use:** Unusual flotsam can contain a human story: damaged equipment, sealed containers, household objects, fishing gear, or a drifting freezer.

**Rules:** Treat containers as unknown until safely inspected. Smell, contamination, sharp edges, entanglement, and unstable cargo are credible hazards. Never require bare-hand interaction with hazardous contents. The freezer story may inspire one authored optional recovery incident, not a recurring random gag.

## 6. Isolation psychology and perception

Represent the cognitive workload of long, repetitive, isolated work without diagnosing the protagonist or implying that all seafarers hallucinate.

### 6.1 Grounded effects

- **Fatigue:** sustained work and poor rest can reduce vigilance and increase response time.
- **Circadian disruption:** irregular light exposure and watch schedules can disturb sleep timing and quality.
- **Monotony and sensory deprivation:** a repetitive soundscape and featureless horizon can make small changes unusually salient.
- **Acoustic hypervigilance:** after prolonged listening, ordinary engine, ventilation, wave, and hull noise can be misinterpreted.
- **Spatial uncertainty:** fog, darkness, lack of landmarks, and limited depth cues can reduce confidence in bearings and distance.
- **Irritability and anticipation:** long periods away from home and a strong focus on returning can increase tension. Do not use “channel fever” as a universal clinical diagnosis.

The submarine environment is a useful reference for confinement and watch schedules, but the protagonist operates a small fishing vessel. Use transferable effects—fatigue, monotony, poor sleep, isolation—not submarine-specific claims such as coffin-sized bunks, a fixed 18-hour day, or total lack of daylight.

### 6.2 Gameplay representation

Use one internal **Operational Strain** model rather than adding several overlapping meters. It is separate from Panic:

- Operational Strain represents fatigue, accumulated workload, poor rest, and sensory monotony.
- Panic represents immediate fear / perceived threat and retains its existing system.
- Strain can make perception less reliable; Panic can amplify threat interpretation.
- Neither value proves that an anomaly is imaginary.

Potential manifestations include slower instrument interpretation, reduced confidence on an uncertain bearing, a brief afterimage after looking at a bright light, a familiar engine rhythm seeming irregular until checked, or a false distant light / sound that disappears when the player verifies it.

**Hard rules:**
1. Never invert steering or movement controls because of hallucination.
2. Never hide a real attack telegraph behind a hallucination.
3. Never make an essential quest clue exist only during a hallucination.
4. Never erase map knowledge, inventory, or player progress as psychological punishment.
5. Give the player a verification method: instrument, second observation, landmark, radio, log, or safe return to harbour.
6. Do not imply that real mental illness makes someone violent or monstrous.
7. Keep sensory events short, rare, and context-dependent; silence is often stronger than constant voices.

## 7. Regional placement guide

| Region | Suitable phenomena / experiences | Exclusions and cautions |
|---|---|---|
| Greyhaven | Hull knocks, drifting debris, false navigation lights, fatigue after long work, ordinary harbour sound | No deep-sea brine pool, brinicle, or vent field |
| The Shallows | Water-colour fronts, bioluminescent plankton, reef animal calls, current boundaries, damaged fishing gear | No abyssal gigantism or deep scattering layer as a seabed |
| Blackrock Reach | Surge channels, basalt abrasion, line snags, hull impacts, unusual echoes around cliffs | Physical wave / current hazards must remain readable |
| The Drowned Parish | Acoustic reflections, flooded structures, silt clouds, lost equipment, localised low visibility | Do not make every echo supernatural |
| The Outer Shoals | Deep scattering layer, diel vertical migration, pelagic bioluminescence, drifting vessels, longline anomalies | Do not turn every sonar return into a predator |
| The Hollow Trench | Brine pools, gas hydrates, cold seeps, hydrothermal vents where geology permits, whale falls, Osedax, deep-sea gigantism, rare acoustic events | Brinicles excluded from current warm-water baseline; deep hazards need survey and warning rules |

## 8. Discoverable records and journal rules

Every investigation record should include record ID and title; author / source identity if known; date and region if known; evidence type and confidence; instrument or observation method; direct observation; witness inference; plausible ordinary explanations; contradictory evidence; unresolved questions; and verified / disputed / uncorroborated status.

The journal must distinguish **observation** from **interpretation**. Example:

- Observation: “The return appeared 140 m above the charted bottom at 22:14.”
- Interpretation: “Possibly a migrating scattering layer.”
- Unresolved: “The second return persisted after the first weakened.”

Never rewrite an uncertain record as a confirmed monster entry merely because the player found it frightening.

## 9. Production and implementation boundary

This document defines design and content rules. It does not claim these systems are implemented in Unity.

Recommended implementation order:
1. Author the DSL as a time-of-day-dependent acoustic volume.
2. Add a brine-pool survey location with safe standoff and instrument feedback.
3. Add one vent / seep ecology vignette with distinct visual and audio identity.
4. Add whale-fall succession and Osedax as a non-hostile ecological discovery.
5. Add a small set of line / hull / debris incidents to the existing maritime atmosphere director.
6. Add Operational Strain only after rest, voyage duration, and fatigue counterplay are designed.
7. Add fictional acoustic anomalies only after the ordinary sound library is established.

All systems should use stable event IDs, deterministic opportunity evaluation, save/load persistence where state matters, and accessibility equivalents for critical cues.
