# Regional Ecology and Creature Distribution

> Project: WHERE THE SOUNDINGS END  
> Status: Proposed worldbuilding baseline — climate and exact species roster remain provisional  
> Owner: Project lead  
> Version: 0.1  
> Last reviewed: 2026-11-10  
> Scope: Regional habitats, ordinary wildlife, ecological activity, dormant life, food webs, and creature placement

## 1. Purpose and canon boundaries

The archipelago must feel like a functioning marine ecosystem before it feels haunted. Wildlife should feed, shelter, migrate, reproduce, compete, evade predators, scavenge, and respond to environmental change whether or not the player is present.

This document assigns ecological identities to the six major regions and their difficult-to-reach sublocations. It is a worldbuilding specification, not an executable simulation or a final species database.

### 1.1 Provisional environmental baseline

For planning, assume a warm-water maritime island arc with:

- A volcanic island chain and tectonically active deep-water margin.
- Tropical to subtropical coastal conditions, with local variation caused by elevation, currents, exposure, and depth.
- Mangrove-fringed river mouths and sheltered bays where suitable sediment and salinity conditions exist.
- Seagrass meadows, coral reefs, rocky volcanic shores, estuaries, and offshore pelagic waters.
- A deep slope, submarine canyons, seamounts, cold seeps, and at least one hydrothermal vent field near tectonically active crust.

The climate remains **provisional**. If the project later selects a colder climate, replace warm-water species and habitats rather than forcing tropical wildlife into an incompatible setting.

### 1.2 Three kinds of creature content

Keep these categories explicit in every creature record:

1. **Real-world reference:** A real animal, ecological process, or habitat used as a factual reference. Cite the source. Do not imply that a composite game creature is a real species.
2. **Game-native natural fauna:** An original species that follows ordinary biological rules. It may be dangerous to fish, territorial, venomous, or capable of injuring a vessel accidentally, but it is not supernatural.
3. **Anomalous entity:** A deliberately unnatural presence with a defined violation of ordinary ecology, observable clues, triggers, limits, and player counterplay. Refer to ANOMALOUS-SPECIES.md.

No animal should attack the player merely because it is labelled a predator. Player-directed hostility requires a species-specific reason such as territorial defense, nest disturbance, prey confusion, injury, habituation, or a supernatural rule.

## 2. Ecological design principles

### 2.1 Habitat determines behavior

A creature's movement, diet, resting place, activity period, and sensory strategy must make sense for its habitat.

- Reef fish use coral heads, crevices, cleaning stations, and feeding territories.
- Seagrass residents graze, browse, hide among blades, or use the meadow as a nursery.
- Estuary species tolerate changing salinity and use tidal channels, mud, roots, and pilings.
- Rocky-shore organisms withstand wave exposure and changing immersion.
- Deep-sea organisms conserve energy, exploit sparse food, use chemical or mechanical cues, and often have slow life histories.
- Seamount and canyon communities concentrate around hard substrate, relief, currents, and food delivery.

### 2.2 Not all life is continuously active

The world needs visible quiet periods. Creatures can be:

- Feeding or grazing.
- Hunting or stalking prey.
- Cleaning at a station.
- Resting in a crevice, den, burrow, or sheltered current.
- Sleeping or showing reduced activity.
- Brooding eggs or guarding a nursery.
- Molting or temporarily hiding while vulnerable.
- Migrating through a corridor.
- Scavenging a carcass or discarded catch.
- Drifting with a current.
- Avoiding heat, wave exposure, low oxygen, or a predator.

A dormant creature is not necessarily asleep. It may be hidden, buried, attached to substrate, brooding, inactive between feeding bouts, or waiting for suitable environmental conditions.

### 2.3 The ecosystem is a network, not a spawn table

Use trophic relationships and habitat suitability to determine where animals appear. A reef predator should be more likely where prey schools and cover exist. A scavenger should respond to carrion and discarded material. A nursery habitat should contain more small juveniles than adult apex predators.

Suggested food-web layers:

| Layer | Ecological roles | Example references |
|---|---|---|
| Primary production | Seagrass, algae, phytoplankton, chemosynthetic microbes | Seagrass meadow, reef algae, vent and seep mats |
| Grazers and filter feeders | Parrotfish, surgeonfish, turtles, bivalves, sponges | Reef, meadow, seep, deep hardground |
| Small consumers | Shrimp, crabs, juvenile fish, zooplankton, worms | Roots, seagrass, reef, water column |
| Mid-level predators | Octopus, morays, groupers, squid, trevallies | Reefs, caves, channels, midwater |
| Large predators | Sharks, billfish, large pelagic fish, deep predators | Outer Shoals, Outer Wall, canyon margins |
| Scavengers and decomposers | Hagfish, amphipods, isopods, crabs, bacteria, bone worms | Wrecks, carcasses, deep seabed |
| Habitat builders | Corals, sponges, oysters, mussels, tube worms | Reef, seamount, seep, vent, estuary |

The same species may occupy several regions if a continuous habitat or migration route connects them. “Unique to a place” should mean a distinctive local population, life stage, behavior, or encounter—not necessarily a species that biologically cannot exist anywhere else.

## 3. Six major regions

## 3.1 Greyhaven — Sheltered Bay and Estuary

**Physical habitat:** Protected volcanic bay, river mouth, brackish channels, muddy flats, pilings, sheltered rocky margins, and mangrove fringe where local conditions permit.

**Resident wildlife**
- Mudskippers and fiddler crabs on exposed mud.
- Mud crabs and burrowing crustaceans around roots and soft sediment.
- Juvenile snappers, mullets, anchovies, and other small schooling fish in shallow channels.
- Oysters, barnacles, mussels, and other attached filter feeders on pilings and submerged structures.
- Egrets, herons, terns, and other shorebirds feeding over mudflats and channels.
- Rays and larger predatory fish entering the bay to forage when water depth and prey availability allow.

**Distinctive game-native resident: Mooring Ray**

A broad, bottom-feeding ray that visits the outer harbor on an incoming tide. It uses its underside sensors to locate buried crustaceans and mollusks, then disturbs sediment while feeding. It is not hostile. It leaves when boat noise, shallow water, or human activity becomes excessive.

**Ecological activity**
- At low tide, mudflat crabs emerge and shorebirds forage.
- On a rising tide, small fish move into flooded channels and mangrove roots.
- At dusk, plankton and small crustaceans become more active in sheltered water.
- Larger predators may follow fish into the bay, then retreat as water depth or prey availability changes.
- Attached filter feeders remain in place and respond to immersion and water flow.

**Dormant and shelter behavior**
- Fiddler crabs retreat into burrows as the tide or temperature changes.
- Oysters and other bivalves close or reduce feeding during unfavorable exposure.
- Small fish shelter among roots, pilings, and submerged debris.
- The Mooring Ray rests on soft bottom outside the busiest harbor area.

**Player/ecology interaction**
Repeatedly fishing one channel can reduce local catch rates. This should recover over time and through migration; do not permanently empty the harbor. Discarded bait and fish scraps attract scavengers and may also draw larger predators toward the harbor mouth.

**Safety rule:** Greyhaven's central harbor is a reliable safe zone. Natural wildlife may pass through, but no full hostile chase encounter should begin inside the protected harbor.

## 3.2 The Shallows — Seagrass, Reef Flat, and Sand Channels

**Physical habitat:** Broad island shelf with seagrass beds, patch reefs, sandy gutters, coral heads, shallow lagoons, and tidal channels.

**Resident wildlife**
- Parrotfish and surgeonfish grazing on algae and reef surfaces.
- Green sea turtles grazing on seagrass where suitable meadows exist.
- Juvenile reef fish sheltering within the seagrass canopy.
- Shrimp, small crabs, sea cucumbers, sea stars, urchins, anemones, and mollusks.
- Day octopuses hunting crabs, mollusks, and small fish around reef heads and rocky crevices.
- Reef sharks and larger predatory fish passing through rather than constantly patrolling for the player.
- Cleaner wrasses and cleaner shrimp operating at localized cleaning stations.

**Distinctive game-native resident: Ribbon Grazer**

A large, slow-moving herbivorous fish that travels in small groups along the edge of seagrass beds. It crops algae and soft growth from the meadow, pauses to feed, and retreats into deeper channels when disturbed. It is a visible indicator of a healthy shallow ecosystem.

**Ecological activity**
- Grazers feed during suitable light and current conditions.
- Small fish use seagrass as shelter and nursery habitat.
- Octopuses forage across reef patches, sometimes carrying prey back to a den and leaving discarded shells near the entrance.
- Cleaning stations become temporarily busy when reef fish queue or hover for parasite removal.
- Coral spawning can occur as a rare seasonal event, synchronized by environmental cues such as temperature and lunar timing. It should not happen every full moon by default or be treated as a universal coral rule.
- At night, some reef fish shelter in crevices and some parrotfish species produce a mucus sleeping cocoon.

**Dormant and shelter behavior**
- Fish shelter among coral branches and rock gaps after dark.
- Octopuses occupy dens and may block or camouflage the entrance.
- Grazers rest in deeper, calmer patches between feeding bouts.
- Coral colonies remain attached and may release reproductive material only during a short environmental window.

**Player/ecology interaction**
Repeated anchoring or dragging gear through seagrass damages habitat and reduces shelter for juvenile fish. Fishing pressure should affect local prey availability, while careful navigation and reduced disturbance allow recovery.

## 3.3 Blackrock Reach — Basalt Coast and Surge Channels

**Physical habitat:** Exposed volcanic shore, basalt shelves, sea stacks, fissures, wave-cut caves, surge channels, and steep nearshore slopes.

**Resident wildlife**
- Moray eels using rock cracks as ambush shelters.
- Octopuses occupying dens beneath boulders and ledges.
- Spiny lobsters and crabs sheltering in crevices.
- Sea urchins and grazing snails on exposed rock.
- Anemones, barnacles, limpets, and other organisms adapted to wave exposure.
- Triggerfish and other reef-associated fish feeding around rock and reef structure.
- Seabirds diving on baitfish concentrated by currents.

**Distinctive game-native resident: Basalt Crawler**

A heavily armored bottom-dweller that feeds on mollusks, small crabs, and carrion. It moves between cracks during calmer periods and wedges itself into rock during heavy surge. It is not a boat hunter; a cornered individual may defend its shelter.

**Ecological activity**
- Wave surge washes plankton and small prey across exposed rock.
- Morays wait in crevices and make short ambush strikes.
- Octopuses search dens and feeding grounds, then return to shelter.
- Lobsters emerge more often during low-light periods.
- Seabirds dive when baitfish schools are pushed toward the surface.
- Small organisms shift into deeper crevices during intense wave conditions.

**Dormant and shelter behavior**
- Lobsters and crabs retreat into deep crevices during daylight or disturbance.
- Morays reduce movement while sheltering with only the head visible.
- Octopuses close or camouflage den entrances.
- Urchins remain attached and use rock cracks as refuge from wave impact and predators.

**Player/ecology interaction**
The coast is dangerous because of rocks, wave energy, and limited maneuvering room—not because every animal is aggressive. A creature displaced from a den should first retreat, posture, or defend its immediate refuge before escalating.

## 3.4 The Drowned Parish — Flooded River Valley and Submerged Settlement

**Physical habitat:** Former river valley flooded by relative sea-level rise or local subsidence. It contains brackish channels, submerged structures, silted streets, drowned trees, mud, masonry, and isolated dry or intertidal remnants.

**Resident wildlife**
- Eels and other crevice-using fish around foundations and broken walls.
- Mullet and juvenile fish using channels as feeding and nursery habitat.
- Mud crabs, shrimp, polychaete worms, and other sediment feeders.
- Oysters, barnacles, mussels, and sponges colonizing masonry and submerged timber.
- Catfish or other bottom-feeding fish in appropriate brackish conditions.
- Shorebirds feeding on exposed sediment during low water.
- Octopus or other opportunistic predators occupying stable cavities.

**Distinctive game-native resident: Parish Eel**

A long-bodied crevice predator that lives among submerged walls and culverts. It feeds on fish, crustaceans, and carrion, then returns to a shelter route. Its ordinary feeding calls and movement should be distinct from any supernatural bell-like sound.

**Ecological activity**
- Tidal movement exchanges brackish and marine water through streets and channels.
- Small fish use the submerged settlement as a maze of nursery shelter.
- Predators patrol openings between buildings.
- Filter feeders gradually colonize exposed surfaces.
- Detritivores and scavengers process organic material collected among foundations.

**Dormant and shelter behavior**
- Eels remain inside cavities between feeding events.
- Crabs bury themselves in soft sediment.
- Small fish form dense sheltering schools inside roofless rooms and broken arches.
- Sessile colonies remain attached to masonry and filter passing water.

**Player/ecology interaction**
The player can disturb fragile settlement habitat by collision, anchoring, and repeated dredging. Do not make every old building a monster nest. Most are simply homes, nurseries, feeding structures, and historical evidence.

## 3.5 The Outer Shoals — Offshore Ridges and Migratory Grounds

**Physical habitat:** Submerged volcanic highs, reef platforms, seamount-like features, isolated pinnacles, strong currents, and open water.

**Resident and migratory wildlife**
- Trevallies, jacks, tuna, and other schooling pelagic predators.
- Smaller baitfish and squid aggregating where current and nutrient conditions concentrate food.
- Reef sharks and oceanic sharks using feeding corridors.
- Manta rays or other large filter-feeding animals where plankton productivity supports them.
- Sea turtles crossing between feeding and nesting habitats.
- Seabirds tracking surface schools and feeding opportunities.
- Deep-water corals and sponges attached to suitable hard substrate below the photic zone.

**Distinctive game-native resident: Longwake Manta**

A large filter-feeding ray that follows plankton-rich current boundaries. It may pass near the boat, circle through a feeding patch, and disappear into deeper water. It is neutral and should never behave like a boss simply because of its size.

**Ecological activity**
- Currents and topographic relief create localized upwelling and eddies.
- Plankton concentrations attract small fish; larger predators follow.
- Migratory schools move along recurring corridors.
- Feeding frenzies may occur when baitfish are compressed near the surface.
- Seabirds reveal schools or surface activity from a distance.
- Some pelagic species gather seasonally for feeding or reproduction.

**Dormant and shelter behavior**
Open-ocean animals rarely have a fixed cave-like shelter. Rest can take the form of reduced movement, deep-water travel, or remaining in favorable current conditions. Do not give all pelagic animals a permanent home point.

**Player/ecology interaction**
Fishing pressure can disperse a school and reduce catch probability locally, but schools should return along migration routes when conditions allow. The player can follow bird activity, surface disturbance, and Sounding Rig readings to locate moving food webs.

## 3.6 The Hollow Trench — Deep Slope, Abyssal Basin, and Hadal Margin

**Physical habitat:** Steep volcanic and tectonic slopes, submarine canyon mouths, deep basin, cold seeps, possible hydrothermal fields, and a deepest trench section.

**Resident wildlife**
- Grenadiers and other deep-sea bottom-associated fish.
- Snailfish in suitable deep and hadal habitats.
- Hagfish and amphipods scavenging carrion.
- Deep-sea octopuses on rocky canyon walls and boulder-strewn seabeds.
- Brittle stars, sea cucumbers, sponges, anemones, and deep-sea corals.
- Midwater fish, squid, and gelatinous animals moving through the water column.
- Specialized seep and vent communities where the correct chemical conditions exist.

**Distinctive game-native resident: Trench Drifter**

A slow-moving deep-water scavenger that cruises near the bottom and detects chemical cues from injured animals or carrion. It does not pursue healthy boats. When food is absent, it conserves energy and drifts with deep currents.

**Ecological activity**
- Marine snow supplies a persistent but sparse food source.
- Scavengers converge on carcasses and discarded organic matter.
- Some deep-water animals move vertically through the water column on daily cycles.
- Large carcasses create temporary food-rich habitats that can support different communities over years or decades.
- Seep and vent communities rely on chemical energy rather than sunlight.
- Deep-sea octopuses may use stable, oxygenated rock sites for prolonged egg brooding.

**Dormant and shelter behavior**
- Slow metabolism and low movement are valid deep-sea behaviors.
- Scavengers may remain inactive until chemical cues indicate food.
- Brooding animals may stay fixed to a site for long periods.
- Animals in high-pressure habitats should not behave like ordinary surface fish with only a dark material applied.

**Player/ecology interaction**
The Sounding Rig can reveal topography, acoustic returns, and changes in local activity, but must not identify every species or guarantee the location of a safe route. The Sleeper remains the principal hostile deep-water threat; ordinary trench wildlife should create scale, movement, and ecological credibility around it.

A careless anchor or collision can leave long-lasting damage |
| The Driftglass Fields | Floating macroalgae and drifting debris convergence zone | Small fish, juvenile pelagic animals, shrimp, crabs, small squid, turtles, and feeding mahi-mahi-like predators use the floating habitat | The habitat moves with wind and current. It can be absent, fragmented, or found far from its previous position |

## 5. Additional ocean-wide ecological events

These events should change what the player sees without requiring every organism to have a complex autonomous simulation.

### 5.1 Tidal feeding windows
At selected tide phases, animals enter channels, feed on newly exposed prey, or retreat from drying habitat. Intertidal creatures should be distributed by their ability to tolerate exposure, desiccation, waves, and predation.

### 5.2 Dawn and dusk migration
Some midwater organisms move vertically through the water column on a daily cycle. At dusk, small fish and squid rise into shallower water to feed; predators follow. At dawn, much of this activity descends again. Treat this as a broad ecological pattern, not a universal rule for every deep-sea animal.

### 5.3 Coral spawning
A rare, authored seasonal window can produce synchronized spawning across compatible coral patches. Environmental cues may include water temperature, lunar timing, and sunset. Spawn clouds attract plankton feeders and create a temporary increase in feeding activity.

### 5.4 Turtle movement
Green turtles can move between offshore juvenile habitats, coastal feeding grounds, and nesting beaches. Adult green turtles primarily graze on seagrass and algae. Use migration as a rare route event, not as a guaranteed daily sighting.

### 5.5 Scavenging and food falls
A large carcass or discarded catch can attract scavengers. A whale fall should progress through phases: mobile scavengers consume soft tissue; enrichment opportunists use remaining organic matter and sediment; later, bone-associated and chemosynthetic communities may persist around the skeleton. A whale fall is a temporary ecological event whose community changes with time.

### 5.6 Upwelling and current concentration
When topography redirects water and causes upwelling, nutrient-rich water can support plankton growth. Baitfish aggregate; larger predators follow; seabirds reveal the surface activity. Do not make upwelling a magical food generator: it should have a location, season, and current pattern.

### 5.7 Molting and brooding
Crustaceans become more vulnerable while molting and may hide in shelters. Brooding creatures may remain near eggs and defend a small area or avoid disturbance. These should be species-specific, not global behaviors.

### 5.8 Storm sheltering
Before and during severe weather, animals may move to deeper water, shelter in crevices, reduce activity, or change feeding grounds. After a storm, debris, displaced animals, sediment plumes, and scavenging opportunities may alter local activity.

## 6. Creature behavior and player interaction

### 6.1 Natural threat rules

Ordinary wildlife should prioritize survival and food. The player is not automatically its prey.

| Situation | Credible first response | Possible escalation |
|---|---|---|
| Boat approaches at distance | Avoid, observe, continue feeding, or change depth | Usually none |
| Boat approaches den or nest | Freeze, retreat, display, or guard local area | Short defensive rush or bite |
| Engine / sonar-like pulse disturbs animal | Startle, flee, shelter, or investigate depending on species | Repeated disturbance may displace it |
| Predator is feeding on prey | Continue feeding or flee only if risk rises | Defensive posture if cornered |
| Predator is injured | Seek cover and reduce activity | Defensive strike only if approached |
| Large animal crosses boat path | Ignore, alter course, or pass beneath | Accidental collision is possible |
| Player follows repeatedly | Increase avoidance distance or abandon route | Species-specific defensive behavior |

Avoid “revenge AI” where an animal remembers the player and hunts them for arbitrary reasons. Persistent memory should reflect a real repeated stimulus: repeated approach to a den, repeated injury, repeated fishing pressure, or a supernatural trigger.

### 6.2 Ecological evidence the player can read

- Empty shells near a den suggest an octopus feeding site.
- Small fish suddenly leaving a reef ledge suggest a nearby predator.
- A cluster of birds diving in the same area reveals a surface baitfish school.
- Freshly disturbed sand indicates recent bottom feeding or a ray's passage.
- New scrape marks and shed crustacean shells indicate movement or molting activity.
- A missing patch of seagrass may indicate grazing pressure or habitat damage.
- Bacterial mats, mussel beds, and tube worms indicate a seep or vent community.
- A slow shift in the Sounding Rig return may reveal migration or changing current rather than a monster.

## 7. Simulation model: what to simulate and what to abstract

For a solo-first Unity game, do not simulate every animal continuously.

### Near the player
Run visible individuals with movement, steering, perception, feeding, avoidance, and species-specific action selection.

### In the nearby region
Use small populations, school agents, feeding patches, and migration routes. Update them at a lower frequency.

### Outside the active region
Use population cohorts, habitat suitability, feeding pressure, spawning windows, and deterministic event outcomes. Do not spawn hundreds of distant individual creatures.

Suggested ecology state variables per habitat patch:

| Variable | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Prey biomass | Available local food |
| Predator pressure | Approximate local predation demand |
| Habitat quality | Suitability of cover, substrate, and water conditions |
| Disturbance | Recent fishing, noise, collision, light, or player intrusion |
| Recruitment | New juveniles joining the population |
| Migration flow | Animals entering or leaving the patch |
| Carrion / detritus | Scavenging opportunity |
| Reproductive window | Whether spawning or breeding is possible |

Simple patch update concept:

- Food availability increases through production, recruitment, and migration.
- Food availability decreases through predation, fishing harvest, and habitat loss.
- Predator activity depends on prey availability, energy, suitability, and risk.
- Population recovery is bounded by habitat quality and reproductive rate.
- A patch cannot recover beyond its authored carrying capacity.

Do not use unrestricted exponential population growth. Use capped recruitment, mortality, and migration. The existing FISH-POPULATION-MODEL.md remains authoritative for fish-stock mathematics.

### 7.1 Performance targets

- Visible animals: individual simulation at the active encounter rate.
- Near-region schools: group-level or low-frequency update.
- Distant species: cohort and event-based simulation.
- Tides, lunar phase, season, weather, and current: shared world inputs, not duplicated per creature.
- Save stable population deltas and important named individuals; do not serialize every decorative fish.

## 8. Content constraints and quality tests

1. A species must have a habitat, food source, predator or avoidance relationship, activity profile, and shelter strategy.
2. A dormant or resting state must be visible or inferable through clues.
3. Not every region needs a hostile enemy.
4. A large animal is not automatically a boss.
5. A predator must have prey and a reason to hunt.
6. A nursery site must contain small or juvenile life stages.
7. A spawning event must have environmental cues and a limited window.
8. A scavenger must have a food source.
9. A migratory species must have a route and a reason to move.
10. A supernatural presence must be documented separately from real ecology.

Acceptance tests:
- A player can observe at least three non-hostile wildlife activities in a region.
- Disturbing a den, nursery, or feeding site produces species-appropriate responses.
- Wildlife returns or redistributes after temporary disturbance.
- Fishing pressure changes local availability without permanently deleting a region's food web.
- The same species behaves differently in feeding, resting, migration, and shelter conditions.
- The game does not make all creatures attack the player.
- Ecological activity remains legible when the player is not directly looking at the animals.

| The Blue Maw | **Pale-thread Eel** | Low-activity cave predator that hunts small crustaceans and fish along the sinkhole wall | Remains in a narrow crack during bright or turbulent periods | The player can detect its route through small prey movements and repeated shallow acoustic returns |
| The Siltfall Scar | **Siltmantle Grenadier** | Slow bottom-associated scavenger that patrols the margin of sediment deposits and feeds on carrion and small invertebrates | Rests close to the bottom with reduced movement when food cues are absent | Its approach can indicate a recent food fall; its movement may precede a sediment cloud |
| The Outer Wall | **Edgefin Tuna** | Fast pelagic predator that tracks baitfish along the shelf break and current fronts | Continues broad-ranging movement rather than using a fixed den; activity follows prey availability | Surface strikes and baitfish compression reveal a feeding front; it should not attack the boat |
| The Broken Caldera | **Ashfin Cardinalfish** | Small cave-associated fish that schools in cooled lava cavities and emerges to feed in low light | Hides deep inside cavities during daylight or when larger predators approach | A school withdrawing into the lava tubes can foreshadow a predator entering the caldera |
| The Last Anchorage | **Anchorback** | A recurring mature green-turtle-like individual that returns to a sheltered seagrass patch to feed and rest | Rests quietly in a protected underwater pocket between feeding bouts | A non-hostile recognizable wildlife encounter; repeated visits can show habitat recovery or disturbance |
| The Bone Orchard | **Bonepicker Isopod** | Scavenging crustacean that detects carrion and joins other scavengers at large food falls | Remains buried or inactive when food is scarce; gathers around new carrion | Its arrival indicates a recent carcass or food fall; populations shift as the carcass is consumed |
| The Black Chimneys | **Cinderlip Vent Shrimp** | Game-native vent-associated shrimp that grazes microbial films and occupies the cooler outer margins of a vent community | Clusters in stable temperature and chemical gradients; retreats from plume changes | Its distribution can indicate which side of a vent field is less chemically extreme |
| The Lantern Drift | **Lantern-thread Squid** | Small midwater squid that follows prey and participates in nightly vertical movement | Descends with the deeper scattering layer during daylight | Its synchronized movement helps the player distinguish a biological layer from an anomalous light pattern |
| The Sulfur Orchard | **Siphon Crown Tubeworm** | Seep-associated tube worm supported by symbiotic microbes that use chemical energy | Remains anchored to the seep substrate; retracts its exposed crown when disturbed | Dense patches reveal stable seep conditions; damaged patches recover slowly |
| The Hanging Garden | **Veilfin Octopus** | Deep-water octopus that hunts small crustaceans and uses coral and sponge structure as cover | A brooding female remains at a stable, oxygenated site and guards eggs for an extended period | The player can discover a long-term brooding site; disturbing it should have lasting ecological and ethical consequences |
| The Driftglass Fields | **Raftclaw Crab** | Small crab that lives among floating macroalgae and feeds on attached organisms and trapped organic matter | Hides deep in the raft when birds or larger fish approach | Its presence helps establish that floating algae is a living habitat, not disposable scenery |

### 8.2 Local species distribution rules

- Do not place every signature creature in every visit. Use habitat suitability, time, tide, weather, season, and disturbance.
- A named individual such as Anchorback should persist across visits. Do not respawn it as a disposable random animal.
- A breeding or brooding animal should have a stable site and a clear recovery or abandonment rule.
- Schooling animals should move as a group with spacing, compression, split, and reunion behaviors rather than as dozens of independent random fish.
- A predator's presence should be supported by prey availability. If its prey population moves away, the predator should follow, reduce activity, or leave.
- Deep-sea organisms should have slower movement and fewer unnecessary high-energy actions.
- Avoid placing fragile corals, tube worms, or sponge gardens on exposed high-energy surfaces where their habitat would be implausible.
