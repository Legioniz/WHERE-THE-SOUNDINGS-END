# The Outer Shoals — Migratory Grounds and Offshore Ridges

> Project: WHERE THE SOUNDINGS END  
> Status: Proposed regional design — separate survey sandbox exists; travel network pending  
> Owner: Project lead  
> Version: 0.2  
> Last reviewed: 2026-11-14

## Regional identity

The Outer Shoals are a wide offshore system of submerged ridges, deep gutters, scattered hardgrounds and migratory corridors. They sit beyond the familiar coastal landmarks. A journey here asks the player to manage distance, fuel, exposure, local conditions and the decision to turn back before the return route becomes uncertain.

The shoals are not a single island-like reef. Their depths and bottom types change across a broad area. A chart made during one survey is useful but cannot guarantee that every shallow feature remains accurately represented after sediment movement and storms.

## Landmarks and places

- **The Outer Marker:** the last familiar harbour reference in the opening story; its exact coordinates and physical design remain to be fixed.
- **The Long Ridge:** a submerged hardground that redirects currents and concentrates some migrating life.
- **The Blue Gut:** a deeper channel between shoal systems; a useful route reference once surveyed.
- **The Drift Line:** a corridor of floating weed, debris and surface life that changes with wind and current.
- **The Quiet Reach:** a broad stretch with few visual landmarks; it should challenge the player to use repeated observations rather than a magic compass.

These are proposed names and not yet chart data. Unknown locations must remain absent from the player's chart until physically visited or discovered through the established chart/Sounding Rig rules.

## Ecology

The Outer Shoals connect coastal feeding grounds with more exposed pelagic water. Migratory fish, schooling bait, seabirds, turtles and larger predators may pass through according to season, current and prey. A rare large animal may appear as part of migration or feeding, not as an obligatory boss.

The player should sometimes encounter a rich fishing ground with no anomaly at all. Ecological success and supernatural escalation are separate systems.

## Narrative use

The region widens the mystery. An acoustic pattern or physical clue appears far from the Greyhaven coast, but the player cannot assume that similarity proves a single source.

Evidence may include:
- a damaged survey buoy with an intact date plate;
- a route note that predates the Orison;
- two vessels' records that describe a similar timing pattern from different headings;
- a witness account that disagrees about where the sound originated.

At least one ordinary explanation must remain plausible until later corroboration. The player should learn that a pattern can be geographically repeated without revealing its cause.

## Sound and visual identity

- A wide horizon, low islands or no land in view, shifting cloud and moving surface bands.
- Passing schools and seabirds create occasional natural sound events.
- Wind and engine noise become important navigational references.
- Long intervals of open water are allowed; the region should not be filled with constant points of interest.

## Hazards and return

The main threats are range, weather, uncertain shoal depth, changing current and reduced shelter. Every major expedition needs a believable turnaround point and fuel/risk assessment. The player can retreat without losing required story evidence.

## Story role

The Outer Shoals form part of Act II. They connect the present missing-boat incident to older survey history and test the player's willingness to compare multiple sources. They should not function as a straight corridor to the Hollow Trench until regional travel is designed.

## Acceptance questions

- Does a long crossing feel meaningfully different from a nearshore trip?
- Does the player have enough landmark and survey information to make informed decisions?
- Are migration and ecological activity distinct from the anomaly system?
- Can the player return safely after deciding not to continue?
