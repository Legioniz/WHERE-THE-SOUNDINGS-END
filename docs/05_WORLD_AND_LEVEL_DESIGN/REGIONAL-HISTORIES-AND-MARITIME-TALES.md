# Regional Histories and Maritime Tales

> Project: WHERE THE SOUNDINGS END  
> Status: Proposed worldbuilding expansion — not runtime implementation  
> Owner: Project lead  
> Version: 0.1  
> Last reviewed: 2026-11-14

## Canon boundaries

This atlas deepens the six regions as one connected human and geological geography. It is subordinate to the World Bible, Timeline and regional briefs. The island chain is an unnamed volcanic arc; no real-world country, exact latitude, language group or single cultural analogue is assigned. All new stories and locations are proposed until approved. The Choir remains a human label for unresolved acoustic anomalies, not a confirmed creature or explanation for ordinary events.

## 1. The chain before the mystery

Volcanic construction formed the hard basalt coast and deep margin. Cooling fractures, wave erosion and slope failure produced cliffs, sea caves, surge channels and submerged ledges. Sediment accumulated in sheltered water and moved through channels during storms. Uplift, subsidence and shoreline migration altered the lower inlet and shallow shelf. Charts therefore age; a safe passage can become uncertain without anything supernatural happening.

People first used the sheltered bay seasonally. A protected anchorage, freshwater seep, nearshore food and access to offshore grounds made it useful. A landing place gradually became a lower coastal district of homes, storage, work yards and a communal structure. Its earliest remembered history is practical: hauling boats above the tide, drying nets, sharing storm repairs and keeping tallies of fish, debts and missing gear.

As the inlet migrated and storms damaged the lower shore, households moved uphill in stages. Some dismantled houses and carried beams; others left after a bad season; some continued using the old landing after most families had moved. The later name Drowned Parish describes the remains, not necessarily the original settlement name, and does not prove one catastrophic flood. Reused beams, hinges and stone steps connect the old district to present Greyhaven.

## 2. Greyhaven — The harbour that climbed the hill

Greyhaven began as a practical anchorage, not a planned town. Its first permanent community gathered around a freshwater path, drying ground and lower landing. The oldest surviving civic record is a timber tally board: boat names, weights, repair debts and marks showing who helped after a storm. Later generations treated it as a public record because it remembered mutual aid.

The higher village consolidated as the lower inlet became unreliable. The Harbour Office inherited copied pages rather than a complete archive. Entries were recopied, dates omitted and later annotations mistaken for original statements. The west-headland lighthouse was built after repeated wrecks on the exposed approach. Its lamp and bell were separate warning instruments. The lighthouse bell's last confirmed sounding remains Year -11; later maintenance entries do not verify another ring.

**Places:** The Quay Ledger records moorings, weather and missing gear. The Market Floor is where catches are weighed and debts argued; Mara keeps an empty crew crate. The Repair Yard holds Tomas's labelled radios, engine housings and salvaged fittings. The Net Yard is Inez's workplace; her woven birds began as gifts made from line offcuts for children waiting for boats. The Inn Veranda is a weather-waiting place where stories circulate but do not equal measured evidence. The Outer T-pier chart table is a local reference, not proof that distant waters are mapped.

Local sayings include “Bring the tide back with you” (safe return), “A full net does not mean a good day” (catch is not profit) and “Write the weather twice” (verify before changing the harbour board). Greyhaven's tension is not that everyone hides the truth; it is that people have different standards for a reliable account.

## 3. The Shallows — The shelf that feeds the islands

The Shallows are the oldest working ground in local memory. Reef heads, sand gutters, seagrass beds and shallow channels form a changing shelf. Its geography is remembered by routes and water colour rather than fixed borders. Fishers teach the difference between pale sand, darker grass and broken water over reef. Seasonal customs protect nursery areas and avoid dragging gear across living reef.

**Glass Channel** was once the most reliable shallow-draft passage; a storm buried part of its marker line. **Ribbon Meadow** is a seagrass nursery. Fishers say it “breathes” before weather changes, likely noticing current or feeding activity. **Split Reef** has a narrow cut between two heads; one of its paired markers was lost, so safe passage now requires tide and repeated soundings. **Old Marker Line** stakes were copied across generations of charts, making old charts dangerous when treated as current. **Outer Shelf Edge** marks the shift from visual navigation to instrument checks.

The **Green Lantern School** is a story about pale moving flashes over the meadow. Children are warned not to follow them beyond the marked channel; they may be feeding fish, disturbed plankton or moonlight on surface chop. **The Reef That Answers** tells of a hull knock returned from Split Reef. Its practical version is an echo that changes with heading and distance. The Shallows teach the player to establish ordinary baselines before interpreting an anomaly.

## 4. Blackrock Reach — The coast that taught distance

Blackrock Reach is the exposed volcanic side of the chain. Basalt fractures, undercut cliffs, sea caves and surge channels are products of cooling, erosion and wave energy. The coast was too exposed for a large permanent settlement, though seasonal camps used temporary lee pockets. Crews gathered driftwood, collected shellfish in calm conditions and retrieved wreck material when conditions allowed.

**Split Face** is a cliff fracture with a pale mineral seam once used as a daylight bearing; rockfall changed its outline. **Needle Stack** disappears in spray and fog, creating the impression that it moves. **Backwash Cut** is where reflected waves meet incoming swell; steering error has caused more damage here than any mysterious force. **Glass Cave** has a bright entrance and dark interior pool, but is unsafe to enter by boat when swell changes. **Lee Shelf** is temporary refuge, not a permanent safe harbour.

**The Second Knock** began with a repair crew hearing a second impact after hammering a hull near Glass Cave. Their report traced it to reflected sound. Later versions claim the knock continued after the hammer stopped, but no first-hand record confirms that addition. Blackrock tests echo, distance and exposure; the cliffs need not be sentient to be dangerous.

## 5. The Outer Shoals — Routes that do not stay put

The Outer Shoals are submerged ridges, sediment channels, hardgrounds and migratory corridors, not one continuous reef. Currents accelerate over ridges and slow in gutters. Floating weed gathers into temporary lines. Fishers and survey crews learned routes by comparing depth, drift, bird activity, surface colour and elapsed time. A single sounding could be wrong; repeated passes on different headings were more useful.

**Outer Marker** is the last familiar reference maintained by Greyhaven, not the edge of known water. **Long Ridge** gathers current edges and baitfish, though its shallowest line remains uncertain. **Blue Gut** is a deeper passage whose current can carry a vessel off a straight course. **Drift Line** is mobile weed, foam and surface life. **Quiet Reach** has few visual references, not an absence of wildlife or sound.

**The Three Bearings** concerns a surveyor who recorded three ridge edges from three headings rather than drawing one confident line. **The Empty Buoy** was recovered with its lamp and date plate intact but anchor chain separated. The official report listed wear or snagging; a later copy added a sound heard after recovery, absent from the original. This region teaches that repeated patterns do not prove one source and copied records can accumulate false detail.

## 6. The Drowned Parish — The district that moved uphill

The Drowned Parish was a lower coastal district abandoned in stages. The inlet migrated, storms damaged the landing and families relocated to higher ground. Some houses were dismantled; some beams reused; other structures remained in occasional use after permanent households departed. “Parish” is a later mariner's label that may preserve a memory of communal gathering or record keeping. It does not establish a particular real-world religion or denomination.

Its bell structure was used for notices, gatherings or storm warnings. This bell is physically distinct from the Greyhaven lighthouse bell unless future evidence proves otherwise. **Old Landing** is a timber-and-stone loading edge with several generations of repairs. **Bell House** changed purpose over time; later walls may enclose an older open shelter. **Sunken Lane** is a route between homes and landing, partly hidden by sediment. **Record Room** contains fragments of lists, receipts and copied notices, not a complete chronicle. **Upper Steps** show through repair layers that relocation was gradual.

Three accounts disagree about the last gathering. One family says the bell called people uphill before a storm. A harbour ledger says the rope had already been removed and runners carried the warning. A salvager describes carrying away a bell-shaped object but cannot identify its origin. The player may compare dates, handwriting, fasteners and the surviving mount without resolving every contradiction. The Parish should not become an exposition room with all answers.

## 7. The Hollow Trench — The margin beyond the chart

The Hollow Trench lies along the deep tectonic margin. Its upper slope is cut by gullies and canyon edges; deeper depressions collect sediment, while hardgrounds and fluid pathways support specialised communities. It is not a single vertical pit. Its history is known mostly through instruments: lead lines that failed to reach bottom, early echo sounders with uncertain returns and later surveys that disagreed about slope position.

**Upper Slope** is the transition from offshore ridge to canyon margin. **Seep Field** is local fluid discharge and specialised life, a geological and ecological habitat. **Brine Boundary** is dense saline water in a depression, not a portal. **Fall Site** shows whale-fall succession. **Last Reliable Return** is a survey term for the edge of repeatable bottom confidence, not a physical wall.

The **Rule of Three Returns** is a surveyor's lesson: an initial depth, a second after changing heading and a third after vessel drift are observations under different conditions. A later oral version says the third return came from below the seabed, but no surviving raw record supports it. The Orison's final route crossed this margin. One fragment suggests the crew changed heading to verify a return; another suggests they were trying to reach a marker before weather closed the route. The protagonist survived, but no account establishes the crew's final actions.

## 8. Stories carried between ports

These are original in-world tales, not adaptations of identifiable sacred stories from real cultures. Each has a practical telling and a later embellishment.

- **The Borrowed Voice:** a radio operator hears a familiar phrase on an empty frequency. It contains no new information. Cross-talk, receiver damage and memory under stress remain plausible. The danger begins when someone obeys it.
- **The Lantern That Waits:** a light appears beyond Outer Marker and vanishes on approach. It may be a vessel changing bearing, a buoy obscured by swell, refraction or fatigue. Never steer toward an unverified light.
- **The Net With No Weight:** a wet, properly set net comes aboard nearly empty. Stories say something passed through it; fishers check current, mesh, timing and school movement first.
- **The Wake Against the Wind:** a wake seems to move against surface drift. It may be a current seam, another vessel or perspective error; surface drift and subsurface current differ.
- **The Name Left Off the Board:** missing crew names remain on the harbour board until families ask for removal. It is a custom about grief and uncertainty, not a supernatural event.
- **The Bell Below:** crews report a bell over the Parish at slack tide. Others insist the bell was removed. The story cannot establish that it survives or that it is the lighthouse bell.
- **The Last Reliable Return:** a surveyor marks the final repeatable sounding and leaves the next line blank. The sea did not take the number; the instrument could no longer support a confident answer.

## 9. Narrative and implementation rules

A tale is a lead, not proof. Record who told it, where they heard it and what they directly observed. Keep original records, copies, annotations and embellishments distinct. Do not turn every story into a quest, collectible, monster or supernatural encounter. Allow physical explanations to be tested. Keep ordinary ecology independent from the Choir. An unverified voice must never provide objective information or safe navigation instructions. Unknown locations remain absent from the chart until existing discovery rules permit them. Some places should be quiet, ordinary and without a legend.

This atlas is proposed narrative content. It does not create playable travel, chart markers, quest state, collectibles, journal entries, dialogue nodes, scene geometry, assets or audio. Each location needs spatial bounds, route and depth, ecology, hazards and counterplay, discovery gate, soundscape, asset budget and acceptance tests before implementation.
